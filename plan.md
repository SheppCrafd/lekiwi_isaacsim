## What this project actually is

We're teaching a small wheeled robot (LeKiwi) to drive around and avoid orange cones — but
we're teaching it entirely inside a computer simulation first, before we ever touch a real
robot. Once the simulated "brain" (an AI) is good at the task, we build the real robot and
load that trained brain onto it.

We're actually building **two** versions of the brain: one that "sees" using a camera, and
one that "sees" using a spinning laser distance sensor (lidar). Think of it like two
students studying for the same test using different senses — same task, different way of
perceiving the world.

**The steps, in order:** finish checking our 3D robot models → rent a cloud computer with a
graphics card → make sure the simulator actually runs → train the two AI brains → test them
in simulation → buy and build the real robot → move the trained brains onto it → test and
fix in the real world.

## Phase 3 — Lock in the big design decisions

- [x] **Decide to train two separate AI brains — one per sensor.**
  - **What:** Rather than one AI handling both sensors, we're building and training two fully independent brains.
  - **Why:** A camera and a lidar "see" the world in completely different ways, so it's cleaner and more reliable to train each one on its own.
- [x] **Fix a bug in how the robot's starting angle gets randomized.**
  - **What:** Made sure the robot only starts facing a random direction — never tipped over or tilted — same as the real robot always sitting flat on the ground.
  - **Why:** A robot that spawns tilted over doesn't represent anything close to reality.
- [x] **Decide what information the AI is "allowed" to use.**
  - **What:** The AI only ever gets what the real robot's sensors would actually provide (camera image or lidar readings) — never secret information like the exact location of the goal.
  - **Why:** If the AI can cheat by seeing things its real sensors can't detect, it'll learn a strategy that only works in simulation and fails on the real robot.
- [x] **Copy proven technical settings from another working robot-AI project instead of guessing.**
  - **What:** Rather than inventing settings from scratch (simulation speed, the AI training algorithm, the neural network design), we copied them from a real, working open-source robot-AI project.
  - **Why:** Guessing at these numbers risks wasting real training money on settings that don't work. Borrowing from something already proven is safer.

## Phase 5 — Decide how the robot gets rewarded and punished

- [x] **Decide when a practice round ends.**
  - **What:** Every attempt ends after a time limit, driving out of bounds, hitting a cone (fail), or safely settling inside the goal area (success).
  - **Why:** Without clear end conditions, the AI has no way to know when it did something right or wrong.
- [x] **Punish the AI for hitting a cone.**
  - **What:** Hitting a cone gives an explicit negative score.
  - **Why:** Cone avoidance is the whole point of the task.
- [x] **Discourage jerky, twitchy driving.**
  - **What:** Small penalty for making sudden, sharp changes in driving commands from one instant to the next.
  - **Why:** Without this, the AI might learn a technically-successful but violently jerky driving style that would be rough on real hardware.
- [x] **Reward the AI for real progress, not just for finishing.**
  - **What:** Small reward every step the robot gets closer to the goal than it was a moment before (and a penalty for moving away).
  - **Why:** A single reward only at the very end gives the AI almost nothing to learn from early on — this gives it constant feedback the whole time.
- [x] **Give one big reward for actually succeeding.**
  - **What:** A large reward when the robot's entire body is inside the goal area.
  - **Why:** This is the actual goal of the task, so it should be the single biggest reward by far.
- [x] **Require the robot to actually stay in the goal area, not just clip through it.**
  - **What:** The robot has to sit inside the goal area for about half a second before it counts as a real success.
  - **Why:** Stops the AI from "succeeding" by briefly grazing the goal at high speed and bouncing back out.
- [x] **Decide how solid the cones are physically.**
  - **What:** Cones are fixed in place (can't be knocked over or pushed) — touching one is simply detected as a touch.
  - **Why:** Simplest option that matches "hitting a cone should end the round." Knocking cones around wasn't part of the task.

## Phase 6 — Make the simulator throw realistic curveballs at the robot

- [x] **Scatter the cones randomly for every practice run, not a fixed pattern.**
  - **What:** Every one of the ~1,000,000 practice courses gets its own random cone layout.
  - **Why:** If the AI only ever saw one arrangement, it would memorize that pattern instead of actually learning to avoid cones in general.
- [x] **Randomize cone size and shape too.**
  - **What:** Cones vary in size and come in a few different shapes.
  - **Why:** The real world won't hand the robot one exact, uniform cone every time.
- [x] **Randomize where the robot starts on the course.**
  - **What:** The robot's starting spot is picked randomly across the whole open floor, not one fixed corner.
  - **Why:** The real robot won't always start from the exact same spot either.
- [x] **Make the simulated sensors act like real, slightly-flawed sensors.**
  - **What:** Added realistic noise — camera images get grain, slight shake, and motion blur; lidar readings get random error, occasional missed pings, and slight angle wobble, all worse the faster the robot moves.
  - **Why:** A perfectly clean, noise-free sensor in simulation would train an AI that falls apart the moment it meets a real, imperfect camera or lidar unit.
- [x] **Add realistic sensor glitches on top of the basic noise.**
  - **What:** Camera: glare, occasional lens smudges, exposure/color drift, a few stuck pixels. Lidar: occasional false "something's close" misreads and brief frozen readings.
  - **Why:** These are real failure patterns actual hardware shows — training against them makes the AI less likely to panic when it hits one on the real robot.
- [x] **Randomize the sensors' exact mounting position slightly.**
  - **What:** Each practice run nudges the camera/lidar's position and angle by a small random amount.
  - **Why:** Our real sensor mounts won't be machined to identical precision every time — small physical variation is normal.
- [x] **Add realistic reaction delay to the robot's controls.**
  - **What:** Driving commands take a small, randomized amount of time (up to ~0.2 seconds) to actually reach the wheels.
  - **Why:** Real robots aren't instant — there's always some lag between "send command" and "wheels respond."
- [x] **Randomize how much the wheels slip.**
  - **What:** Each run, the robot's effective driving power is randomly reduced a little.
  - **Why:** Real wheels don't always grip perfectly — floors vary, and a little slip is normal.
- [x] **Randomize the background and surroundings around the course.**
  - **What:** Scatter random clutter and vary the room around the course on every run, while the cones/goal themselves stay exactly as designed.
  - **Why:** We want the AI paying attention to the cones and goal, not memorizing one specific simulated room — it should work in any real room.
- [x] **Start training easy and gradually make it harder.**
  - **What:** Early training uses simpler, more spread-out cone layouts; difficulty ramps up gradually as training progresses.
  - **Why:** Throwing the hardest layouts at the AI from step one tends to make training slower or get it stuck — starting easy is a standard, effective approach.

## Phase 8 — Test the trained brains in simulation, before risking real hardware

- [ ] **Test each AI on practice courses it has never seen before.**
  - **What:** Run the finished AI against a batch of courses that were deliberately held back and never used during training.
  - **Why:** This is the honest test of whether it actually learned the skill, rather than just memorizing the courses it trained on.
- [ ] **Look for specific ways the AI might have "cheated" or overfit.**
  - **What:** Check whether it only works on training-style rooms, gets confused by lighting/angle changes, or found a shortcut that scores well without really doing the task.
  - **Why:** An AI can often find a loophole that scores well in training but completely fails to do the intended job.
- [ ] **Do not skip this step and go straight to the real robot.**
  - **What:** Confirm the AI passes this simulated test before it ever touches the physical robot.
  - **Why:** Bugs are cheap to catch in simulation and expensive — or physically damaging — to catch on real hardware.
